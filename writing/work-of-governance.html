The Work of Governance Is Keeping Promises

Why every public governing board exists to help communities make, and keep, ambitious promises to children.

Patrick J. Gavin · July 26, 2026 · Adapted from Substack

As another school year begins, public schools across the country are opening their doors. In some communities, students have already returned to classrooms. Others will welcome them over the weeks ahead. Some schools will greet their very first class of students. Others will begin another year of promises first made years, or even decades, ago.

Whether they govern a traditional school district, a charter school, or another public educational institution, board members and school leaders should treat the beginning of a school year as an opportunity to ask a fundamental question: What is the work of governance?

The answer is not found in opening day logistics, completed checklists, or a successful board retreat. Those things matter. They simply are not the work itself.

For years, the most common questions I heard from school board leaders were about compliance. Can we discuss this outside a posted meeting? Does this email create a public record? Can two trustees attend the same community event? Are we following Robert’s Rules correctly?

These are legitimate questions. Public boards exercise public authority, and open meeting laws, public records laws, ethics requirements, and conflict-of-interest rules protect transparency and trust. An effective board must be lawful.

Governance Is Not the Meeting

But a lawful board can be an awful board.

A board can post every meeting correctly, approve immaculate minutes, respond promptly to every public records request, and still fail to govern. It can avoid difficult evidence, neglect institutional risk, and devote remarkably little attention to the promises the school or system was created to keep.

The meeting is where governance becomes visible, but governance is what the meeting causes. Did the board clarify what matters most? Did it confront a risk before that risk became a crisis? Did it make expectations clearer for the superintendent or chief executive? A motion can pass without producing action. A report can be received without producing understanding. The agenda may have been completed while the work remained unfinished.

Frameworks such as the Texas Education Agency’s Lone Star Governance and the Council of the Great City Schools’ Student Outcomes Focused Governance have fundamentally improved how many governing boards think about their work.

Their central insight is powerful: governance should organize itself around improving student outcomes rather than supervising management’s daily work. Lone Star Governance states the premise directly: student outcomes do not improve until adult behavior changes.

Governance Begins With Public Commitments

Goals are not merely performance targets. They are public commitments about what children should know, be able to do, and become. Constraints are not merely limits on management. They are public declarations about the values that should not be compromised in pursuit of those goals.

Those commitments should not emerge solely from a strategic planning retreat attended by board members and consultants. They should grow from an ongoing conversation with the community the board exists to serve. Families, educators, students, employers, civic leaders, and taxpayers may not agree on every priority, but governance is the work of helping a community identify the outcomes it values most and the principles it refuses to abandon in pursuing them.

Boards do not merely adopt goals. They help communities make promises.

Different Paths to Public Authority

Public school governing boards derive their authority in different ways. Most traditional school boards are elected directly by voters. Most charter school governing boards are self-perpetuating, selecting new trustees under their bylaws as vacancies arise. Depending on state law, authorizers may simply receive notice of those appointments, review them for statutory or contractual compliance, or formally approve new trustees before they begin serving.

Those differences matter, but they do not alter the board’s fundamental responsibility. Whether authority comes through an election or through the public chartering process, governing boards exercise public authority over public institutions educating public school students with public funds.

For elected boards, democratic legitimacy begins at the ballot box. For charter boards, legitimacy begins with the charter itself. Neither ends there. Every governing board must continually earn the confidence of the community it serves.

When the Institution, District or Charter, Is at Risk

The work of governance is fundamentally the same across public education. Every governing board must protect public trust, steward public resources, hire and evaluate its chief executive, establish direction, and insist on improving outcomes for students. The risks those boards manage, however, are not always the same.

Most school districts are presumed to continue. Charter schools operate under a different institutional bargain. They receive greater operational autonomy in exchange for direct accountability. Persistent academic failure, financial instability, legal noncompliance, organizational weakness, or mission drift may ultimately lead to nonrenewal or closure. Their continued existence is never presumed.

That reality changes what charter boards must pay attention to. Enrollment is not merely an operational metric because families must continue choosing the school. Facilities are not merely an administrative concern because an unaffordable lease can jeopardize the institution. Cash flow, special education capacity, cybersecurity, authorizer relationships, succession planning, fundraising, and organizational culture are conditions that make the mission possible.

But conditions are not outcomes.

A charter school can have a balanced budget, a healthy reserve fund, a beautiful building, growing enrollment, positive family surveys, clean audits, orderly compliance systems, and satisfied students and families while still producing student outcomes that are unacceptable. Operational excellence keeps an institution alive. It does not prove the institution is fulfilling its purpose.

Attention Is the Board’s Greatest Resource

Every board says many things matter. The more revealing question is what receives its attention.

If student outcomes matter, the board studies them routinely and asks whether improvement is occurring rapidly enough. If financial stewardship matters, trustees understand the assumptions beneath the budget instead of merely approving the final document. If community trust matters, the board pays attention to enrollment, persistence, complaints, and public confidence.

None of this requires trustees to manage the organization. Oversight is not management. The board establishes direction, articulates goals and constraints, evaluates results, and hires one employee, the superintendent or chief executive. Management determines how those commitments will be fulfilled.

Respecting that distinction should never become an excuse for passivity. Boards should ask difficult questions, not because they distrust management, but because children deserve answers.

The Question After Every Meeting

Boards should ask whether the meeting was properly posted, whether the minutes accurately reflect the discussion, and whether every legal requirement was satisfied. Then they should ask a harder question:

What promise became more likely to be kept because we met?

Did students become more likely to receive the education they were promised? Did the governing board become better able to recognize progress, anticipate risk, or respond to failure? Did today’s decisions increase the likelihood that the community’s aspirations for its children will actually become reality?

Compliance protects the legitimacy of governance. Financial stewardship preserves organizational capacity. Leadership accountability turns priorities into action. Community engagement sustains public trust. Operational excellence creates the conditions under which schools can succeed. None of those, standing alone, is the purpose of governance.

Governance exists so that communities can make ambitious promises to children, and then refuse to lower those promises when the work becomes difficult.

The work of governance is not merely to conduct meetings. It is not merely to keep institutions open. It is not merely to avoid mistakes.

The work of governance is to help communities make promises worth keeping, and then keep them.
