The Scholarship Tax Credit That Might Just Fund a Bus Ride

Clark County just volunteered to test what I flagged a few weeks ago. It probably won’t be the last district to try.

Patrick J. Gavin · August 18, 2026 · Originally published on Substack

A few weeks ago I restacked a piece from BullshitED reframing the federal Education Freedom Tax Credit as a public school opportunity, not just a pipeline to private school tuition. I added my own case for going further:

Transportation is expressly listed as a qualified expense, separate from special-needs services, and the statute is not limited to private-school tuition. Scholarships may fund qualified expenses connected with attendance at public, private, or religious schools. That means these dollars could support transportation for interdistrict or intradistrict public-school choice, provided the transportation is “required or provided by” the school. The law’s new §25F(c)(4) of the Internal Revenue Code incorporates the existing definition of qualified elementary and secondary education expenses in §530(b)(3)(A), the provision governing Coverdell Education Savings Accounts. The strongest model may therefore be an SGO funding transportation that a school or district operates or contracts for, rather than giving families an unrestricted transportation allowance. Many high-performing public schools are sitting on open seats because of enrollment decline. For some families, the barrier is not demand. It is the ride. An SGO designed to fund school- or district-provided transportation to those open seats, while prioritizing students with the greatest need, could be one of the highest-leverage uses of this credit in the entire law.

Today, the theory got a real-world test case.

Clark County School District, the nation’s fifth-largest, with about 280,000 students, is building a scholarship program around this exact credit. That’s also the district I wrote about last week on transportation and school choice.

Rocío Hernández reported in The Nevada Independent that CCSD is partnering with the Las Vegas-based Public Education Foundation to launch what they’re calling the Clark County Scholarship Program. CFO Justin Dayhoff pitched it to the school board this past Thursday, Aug. 13, and didn’t hedge about the district’s motive: “there’s zero reason that school districts should shy away from taking advantage of what could be a substantial revenue opportunity for kids and families.” Or, more bluntly: “It’s our job always to continue to find every opportunity we can to make our pie bigger.”

Public Education Foundation CEO Rich Broome pitched it to donors in similarly direct terms: “Here you say, ‘My $1,700 stays home and is helping our kids get a better education.’”

Let me be careful about what I actually know here, because it’s less than the headline suggests.

We don’t know yet whether CCSD will use this for transportation at all. Dayhoff said the district is waiting on Treasury guidance, expected next month, before deciding whether to award funds directly to families, offer a bundle of services (tutoring, summer school, support for students with special needs), or some combination. Transportation isn’t mentioned in the reporting as a planned use. That’s genuinely unclear right now, not a prediction I’m making.

If it is on the table, though, there’s an obvious place it could plug in. CCSD already runs a small transportation reimbursement pilot for families zoned to a one-star school who use open enrollment to attend a three-, four-, or five-star district school: up to $500 a semester, funded through this year only. An SGO built around this credit could supplement that program directly, extending its reach or its dollar amount without waiting on the next legislative session to appropriate more.

There’s a parallel gap on the charter side that makes the same point. When Nevada set up dedicated transportation funding for charter schools in 2023, demand outran the money almost immediately. By 2025, 23 charter holders were using the grants, the funding was exhausted, and more schools wanted in. The state’s own charter authority estimated it would take roughly $9 million a year just to fully fund the programs already running, without counting new ones. The Legislature came back with more money, but on its own schedule. A privately funded SGO doesn’t have to wait for a legislative session. Depending on how it’s structured, this credit could be a way to ramp up transportation access faster than the appropriations process alone ever will.

None of this is limited to district and charter schools, either. Private schools and microschools run into the same wall: a family’s biggest barrier is often not tuition, it’s getting a kid there and back every day. A credit that can fund transportation regardless of sector could widen physical access to school choice across all of them at once, not just whichever sector gets the most attention in the coverage.

There’s a useful data point on the “is this really just a program for private schools” question, too. Jorge Elorza, CEO of Democrats for Education Reform, made the same case I have on a recent WonkyFolk episode with CharterFolk, and made it more directly than I did. Asked what the credit can actually pay for, he listed it out: “Tutoring. Transportation, computers, internet. And also services for students with special needs.” He was blunt about the label critics reach for: “A lot of people refer to this as a voucher program. This is not a voucher program... This isn’t just for private schools. This is for public school students as well.” DFER’s argument for why Democratic governors should look past the program’s origin is straightforward: the money follows the child, doesn’t touch existing state education funding, and is additive rather than a substitute for it.

That contrast is worth sitting with. Nevada’s attorney general, Aaron Ford, a Democrat running for governor, has publicly criticized the broader federal program, worried it could mean less federal revenue for things like Medicaid. At the same time, a traditional public school district in his own state is building a program around it. A national Democratic education reform organization is making the case for it on the record. That split, inside the same state and the same party, tells you something about how far this has already moved past its original partisan framing.

The question I keep coming back to is the same one I started with. Will any district actually point this at the bus route, not just the bank account? Clark County has the enrollment declines, the open seats, an existing reimbursement program it could plug this into, and now a new funding mechanism. Whether it, or the charter sector next door, or some private school or microschool operator gets there first is the part worth watching.
